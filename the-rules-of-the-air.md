# The Rules of the Air

**Outcome: you know where the line
runs before you switch anything on —
what this guide teaches happily, what
it refuses to teach, and why the law
differs from place to place.** This
chapter is plain talk, not legal
advice; for a real question, ask a
lawyer where you live.

## Why a listening tool needs rules

Kismet only receives. It is tempting
to conclude that receiving cannot be
wrong — a radio is just an ear. But
what Kismet collects is information
about *other people*: the café's
network, the phones in a waiting
room, the names of networks those
phones remember from home. A list of
the networks a phone goes looking
for is a small biography — where its
owner lives, works, travels. Ears are
not exempt from rules just because
they are quiet.

## The line, in plain terms

**Your own networks are your
classroom.** Listening to your own
access points, your own phones, your
own gadgets is the intended use of
this guide, and every recipe in
chapter 12 is built on devices and
networks that belong to the reader.

**Beacons are public speech.** An
access point's beacons are
announcements made to everyone in
range, on purpose. Noticing them,
and noting which networks exist
around you, is the wireless
equivalent of reading signs on
shopfronts. Driving or walking around
logging them — *wardriving*, in the
old word — is widely practised and,
in many places, lawful precisely
because it stops at the announcements.

**Other people's traffic is not
yours.** The moment listening turns
toward the *contents* of someone
else's communication — their data,
their messages, their traffic — you
have left the shopfront and started
reading mail. Laws in many countries
treat intercepting communications
this way as a serious offence,
whether or not a password was in the
way. Kismet's normal view is the
management conversation (names,
addresses, comings and goings), and
this guide deliberately stays there.

**Joining is a different act from
listening.** Connecting to a network
you have no right to use, guessing or
cracking its password, or borrowing
its internet connection is
unauthorised access in most
jurisdictions, full stop. Kismet
cannot do these things and this guide
does not teach the tools that can.

**Interfering is worst of all.**
Frames that knock other people's
devices off their network exist, and
some tools send them. Kismet never
transmits — keep it that way. Jamming
and disrupting radio is illegal in
its own right almost everywhere, on
top of whatever it does to the
victims.

## Permission, and other people's houses

Work networks, a friend's house, a
client's office: if the network is
not yours, get the owner's permission
before you study it, in words a third
party could check. "I look after the
Wi-Fi" is a role; if it is your role,
you already know. If you are
surprised by something you find — a
device that is not what it claims to
be — the neighbourly move is a quiet
word to the owner, not a public post
with their network's name in it.

Two habits keep a listener decent:

- **Do not keep what you do not
  need.** A survey of your own street
  will incidentally name your
  neighbours' networks. That is the
  nature of radio. Publishing the list,
  or keeping it longer than the job
  needs, is a choice, and usually the
  wrong one.
- **Respect the randomised address.**
  Modern phones broadcast under
  made-up, rotating MAC addresses
  precisely so listeners cannot follow
  them around. Treat that as a closed
  door, not a puzzle: this guide does
  not teach defeating it.

## The check

Before any session, say out loud
whose networks you expect to hear
and what question you are answering.
If the honest answer is "everyone's,
and I am just curious what I can
collect," stop. Curiosity has a whole
guide of lawful exercise in front of
it — your own airwaves are busier
than you think.
